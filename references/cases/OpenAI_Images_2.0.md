# OpenAI — ChatGPT Images 2.0 (119 sampled frames, source index to 121 ≈ 60 s)

Note: the sheet labels skip source indices at a few points (#80=081, #104=106, #116=118). Some near-duplicate frames were dropped, so durations below use the source index.

## 1. One-line concept
This is a model launch told as a **children's picture book written entirely in generated images**. Every word of a fairy-tale sentence ("Once upon a time there was a chameleon called Cam [who] couldn't help but fit in… he worried no one would notice him. Then he met Lucy and everything changed… Together [they] could be anything they wanted to be") is **typeset inside a photoreal generated scene** (a magazine masthead, a tea towel, a clock face, a mug, a cereal bowl, a rug, road signs, neon). The product capability being proven is text rendering, photorealism, multilingual type and style range. The followed object is the chameleon, Cam.

## 2. Visual world
- **Ground:** pure white, full frame, the entire film. No texture and no grid. The white *is* the ChatGPT canvas.
- **One image window floats on white** and changes **size and aspect ratio shot to shot**:
  - a small inline square (#1–#2, ~8% of frame width);
  - letterbox 2.35:1 at ~75% width (most shots);
  - a centred ~60% version (#21–#24);
  - portrait 2:3 (#25);
  - a small square (#26, #95);
  - an off-centre square bleeding off the right edge (#96);
  - a split frame (#97);
  - collage grids (#52–#53, #63).

  The shape changing *is* the motion system. There is no camera move inside most images.
- **Type:**
  - The only film-level type is the bookend lock-up "ChatGPT [image] Images 2.0": a grotesk (OpenAI Sans) at medium weight, ~2.5% of frame height, black.
  - Every other word is **diegetic**, set in the image's own typography: Didone masthead "Once", brush script "upon", a green clock-face sans "A TIME", slab "there", 3D letter magnets "WAS", book-spine caps "A CHAMELEON", letters made of cereal "CALLED", a rug-pattern "Cam", a newspaper headline, signage (HIM, NO, WOULD, NOTICE), neon script, sequins "LUCY", an embroidered "to", sticker "wanted".
- **Imagery:** photoreal, warm, saturated, with a lot of green (the chameleon) and pink (Lucy's world in act 3). Lighting varies by scene (golden hour, neon club, playroom).
- **Recurring motifs:**
  - the chameleon *taking on the colour of each surface* (white on the newspaper #27–#31, yellow on the taxi #34, blue on the balloon #36, milk-carton print #37–#39). Camouflage = "couldn't help but fit in";
  - the chameleon perched **on top of** every sign in act 2 (#40–#48).

## 4. Transition inventory
- **Inline image → frame (#2→#4).** The thumbnail acting as a word in the lock-up stretches horizontally (aspect morph from the centre) until it becomes the stage. This is the film's one real morph, and it's **reversed at #114→#115**: the last image collapses back into the inline slot, now showing a *different* image. A bookend with a twist.
- **Hard cut on the word (≈90% of all transitions).** Each image is one word, cut at a 0.5 s cadence. The sentence is the carrier; the eye reads a new word in a new place each beat, so the cut never feels random.
- **Fixed-layout slideshow (#37–#39).** An identical milk-shelf composition, cutting only the language. It proves multilingual rendering *by keeping everything else still*.
- **Window reshaping between cuts:** letterbox → portrait → small square. The cut is masked by the frame changing shape, which also demonstrates aspect-ratio control.
- **Peek-in slide to collage (#51→#52).** A sliver of the next tile appears at the right edge one frame early, then the grid slides in.
- **Collage → tile expands (#63→#64).** One tile of a 4-up grid scales to become the next full shot. It's the "collapse to widget" pattern run in reverse.
- **Split-frame gag (#96→#98).** The frame breaks into two panels so the tongue can travel across a boundary. It's a comic-panel transition.
- **Lock-up → logo (#115→#116).** The words and image clear, leaving the OpenAI mark in the same centre.

## 5. Easy-to-miss details
- The **chameleon is always found in a slightly different place** in the frame (top-right on signs, shoulder, bar). The eye hunts for it every 0.5 s, which makes a riffle of 60+ images feel like a game rather than a slideshow.
- **Tiny in-image motion** exists: the clock hands advance (#9→#10), Cam's head turns (#19→#20), the chameleon walks over the headline (#27→#31), the tongue extends (#89→#90). Each is 1 frame of change. The stills are "breathing".
- **Colour camouflage as a copy joke**: "CAM COULDN'T HELP BUT FIT IN" appears on a newspaper where Cam has become newsprint. The visual literally is the headline.
- Typography variety **is** the demo: Didone, script, clock sans, 3D magnets, cereal, embroidery, neon, sequins, sticker, calendar pen. No two consecutive words share a type style or material.
- Arabic and Polish milk cartons (#38–#39) have correct right-to-left layout and diacritics. The proof is in the details, not a caption.
- **Aspect-ratio changes are timed to the grammar**: portrait appears on a non-word beat (#25), and the square only on character beats (#26, #95). Word beats almost always use the 2.35:1 letterbox.
- The final lock-up swaps the topiary thumbnail (#1) for **cowboy Cam** (#115). The same slot now shows how the character evolved.
- The end logo is tiny and has no wordmark (#116–#119): 2 s of the blossom alone.

## 6. Pacing curve
- A metronomic 0.5 s per image for about 80% of the film.
- Variation comes from **holding** (1–2.5 s) on story beats: "Cam" intro (#19–#20), the newspaper headline (#27–#31), "Then he met" (#54–#56), "and everything changed" (#59–#62), the gym (#64–#66).
- The adventure montage (#67–#94) is the density peak: nothing to read, just joy.
- The climax is the cowboy tongue gag (#95–#98), the only multi-panel mini-sequence.
- Resolution: the calm 0.5 s word rhythm of "Together… could be anything they wanted to be" (#103–#112), a 1 s portrait of the pair, the lock-up and 2 s of logo.

## 7. What makes it premium
- **The copy is typeset in the product's own output.** There isn't a single caption overlay: the film's text *is* the demo. It's the purest version of "the type is the timeline".
- A **character with an arc** (lonely → met Lucy → adventures) instead of a feature list. Each capability (photorealism, text, multilingual, style range, consistency of character across 60 images) is proven incidentally.
- **Character consistency** across scenes and costumes is the hidden headline feature, never stated.
- The white ground plus a single morphing window keeps a 60-image riffle calm. It's the only way that much content doesn't feel like a mood board.

## 8. Reusable techniques
1. **Diegetic word riffle.** Split the sentence into single words. Render each word *inside* a different photographic surface (sign, mug, neon, fabric). Cut every 0.5 s, and hold 1–2.5 s on phrase ends. Rule: consecutive words must differ in material, type style and colour temperature.
2. **Inline thumbnail → stage (bookend).** Open on "Brand [image] Product" with the image at cap height. Stretch its aspect ratio from the centre over 0.5–1 s while the words part and fade; it becomes the stage. At the end, collapse the final image back into the same slot.
3. **Shape-shifting window.** Keep the ground empty. Vary only the image window's aspect ratio and size across cuts (2.35:1 for words, a square for character beats, portrait for texture beats). Width stays within 35–80% of frame.
4. **Fixed-layout language swap.** Hold an identical composition and hard-cut only the language or script of the text (3 × 0.5 s).
5. **Followed mascot as hunt.** Place the recurring character somewhere different in every frame (sign top, shoulder, prop) so the viewer's eye scans each cut. Use this for fast riffles that would otherwise be unreadable.
6. **Wide ↔ tight pairs.** In a riffle, pair a wide with a tight crop of the same scene (#78/#79, #80/#81) for a "camera" feel without any camera move.
7. **Split-panel gag.** For one comic beat, break the window into two panels with different grounds and let one element (a tongue, a line) cross the gutter over 1–2 frames.
